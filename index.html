    About

    Solvent Streets estimates what it costs a city to keep its streets in good repair, and whether today's paving budget is enough. The map shows a city's streets, parking lots and sidewalks. The Financials tab forecasts their condition and repair costs over the next 30 years, and where several cities are loaded, Compare ranks them side by side.

    Where the data comes from

    • Streets, parking lots and sidewalks: OpenStreetMap, the volunteer-built map of the world.
    • Population and housing: the 2020 U.S. Census, block by block.
    • Budgets and pavement condition: each city's own published figures, where it has them. The Config tab lists the sources.

    How the forecast works

    Pavement condition is scored from 0 to 100, the Pavement Condition Index (PCI). In the model every street wears down a little each year, side streets faster than main roads, and repairs cost more the worse a road gets: about $5 per square metre to seal a good road, $50 to resurface a worn one and $150 to rebuild a failed one. The hold-steady budget is what a city would have to spend each year to keep its streets from falling further behind.

    What "solvent" means here

    A city's streets are solvent when its street budget pays for their upkeep: it at least matches the hold-steady budget. Nothing happens to a city that isn't. Its streets just get worse each year it falls short. The Financials headline says what share of the upkeep the city funds, and the tiles below it where its streets are headed at that rate.

    The Solvency Score puts that share on a scale of 0 to 100. A score of 100 means the city spends enough to hold its streets steady; 50 means it would need twice today's budget. A city that cites no budget cannot be scored and shows N/A.

    A city that falls far enough behind reaches its tipping point: the year its postponed repairs add up to a full repair cycle of work, a whole network's worth, more than it could catch up on. The headline names that year only when it comes within the 30-year forecast. How both are computed.

    Map colorings

    The map's Color by menu shades each hexagon by one measure, and its legend says what that measure is.

    • Maintained share (the default): how much of the hexagon is road, parking lot or sidewalk.
    • Maintained area: that pavement's area, as the forecast prices it.
    • Parking spaces: estimated parking spaces.
    • Year-1 need: what the first year of repairs would cost, backlog included.
    • Population, Housing units and Population density: from the 2020 Census.
    • Fire response time: the estimated time for a fire engine to arrive from the nearest station.

    The pavement colorings follow the layers switched on under the gear menu. How each is computed.

    Keep in mind

    • Modelled, not measured. These are planning-grade estimates built on national averages for how fast pavement wears and typical contractor prices, not on a city's own engineering data.
    • Starting condition is often assumed. Where a city cites no pavement survey, every street starts at PCI 85 and the city is marked †. Its score rests on that assumption.
    • OpenStreetMap coverage varies. Where volunteers have mapped few parking lots or sidewalks, those figures run low. City boundaries are OpenStreetMap's too, and can lag recent annexations.
    • Today's dollars. Costs include no inflation.
